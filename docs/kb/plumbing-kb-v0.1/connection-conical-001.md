# Conical Sealing Connection

> Draft canonical unit. A conical seat is a sealing geometry. It must not be
> confused with a tapered pipe thread, even when both appear on the same fitting.

## Short description

A conical connection seals where a male cone, female cone, flare, or conical
elastomeric element meets its matching seat. A nut, thread, clamp, or other
retaining feature generates the axial force that brings the conical surfaces
together.

The cone may be a metal-to-metal seat or may compress a separate elastomeric
seal. The exact construction determines what can be reused, lubricated, or
tightened.

## Components

A conical assembly may contain:

- a male cone, flare, nose, or conical insert;
- a matching female cone or recessed seat;
- a nut, sleeve, union, or threaded retainer that applies axial force;
- a pipe end, tube flare, fitting body, or valve port;
- an optional O-ring, conical elastomeric seal, or support ring;
- shoulders, guides, washers, or centering features.

The angle, diameter, material, and surface finish of the matching pair are part
of the sealing design. A visually similar cone from another standard may not
fit or seal correctly.

## How the mechanical connection is created

The conical parts are aligned and brought together along their common axis. The
retaining nut or other mechanism applies axial force so the cone seats evenly
and remains engaged under service load.

The pipework must be aligned independently. The retainer should not be used to
pull a bent or incorrectly positioned pipe into the cone, because side load can
create uneven contact or damage the seat.

The thread or clamp provides engagement and clamping. The conical interface or
its specified elastomeric element provides the fluid barrier.

## What actually seals

The seal is created by the designed conical contact or by an elastomeric seal
compressed within the conical geometry. A metal cone requires compatible
surfaces, correct alignment, controlled loading, and undamaged contact areas.

Some conical fittings use a separate O-ring or gasket. In that case the
elastomer, not an arbitrary metal contact area or the retaining thread, is the
primary sealing element.

## What does not seal

Do not assume that the following is the primary sealing surface:

- a tapered pipe thread located near the cone;
- the retaining nut thread;
- a cone from a different standard or angle;
- a flat washer placed outside the designed seat;
- thread tape or paste applied to a connection designed to seal on the cone;
- extra tightening after the cone or its elastomeric seal is damaged.

If the fitting has both a threaded port and a conical seat, classify the two
interfaces separately.

## Permitted movement and tightening

The cone should seat concentrically without rocking, scraping, or forcing. The
retainer should engage smoothly by hand at first. Once assembled, the conical
parts should not move in normal service.

Do not rotate a seated cone under load unless the product design permits it. Do
not use unlimited tightening to compensate for a mismatched angle, damaged
surface, wrong seal, or pipe misalignment. Product-specific tightening
instructions take precedence over generic rules.

## Typical failures

Typical failure modes include:

- mismatched cone angle, diameter, or connection standard;
- scratched, dented, cracked, corroded, or contaminated sealing surfaces;
- incomplete seating, cross-threading, or missing retainer parts;
- pipe side load or angular misalignment;
- over-tightening that deforms the cone, fitting, or elastomer;
- under-tightening that leaves insufficient contact or compression;
- damaged, aged, displaced, or incompatible conical elastomer;
- a nearby threaded or gasketed joint mistaken for the conical source.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms include:

- leakage at the cone, flare, nut, or valve port;
- leakage after disassembly and reassembly;
- leakage that changes with retainer position but not with thread sealant;
- a joint that reaches its expected orientation too early or too late;
- visible scoring, uneven contact, or a displaced elastomer after inspection;
- a persistent leak despite additional tightening.

The lowest wet point is not necessarily the cone. Trace the first wet location
before assigning the leak to the conical seat.

## Diagnosis

When the connection can be isolated and depressurized safely:

1. Isolate the relevant branch and remove stored pressure and heat.
2. Dry the area and locate the first wet point under the lowest safe test
   condition.
3. Identify whether the assembly contains a metal cone, a separate elastomer,
   a tapered thread, or more than one sealing interface.
4. Record the pipe and fitting markings, orientation, and any change observed
   when the retainer is moved without adding force.
5. If disassembly is justified, inspect the cone, seat, retainer, seal,
   alignment, debris, and assembly order.
6. Compare the cone standard and replacement parts with the manufacturer’s
   documentation; visual similarity is not enough.

If the cone type, thread standard, isolation state, or replacement seal is
uncertain, stop before applying more torque or sealant.

## Assembly errors

Common errors include:

- forcing mismatched cones together;
- allowing dirt, chips, old seal fragments, or corrosion into the seat;
- starting the retainer at an angle or cross-threading it;
- omitting a specified O-ring, support, washer, or guide;
- applying thread sealant to the conical sealing interface;
- turning or tightening the cone while pipework is under side load;
- reusing a deformed or damaged metal cone or elastomer;
- setting final pipe orientation by over-tightening.

## Safety

Isolate and depressurize the relevant system before loosening a conical joint.
Protect against hot water, stored pressure, and sudden release of the pipe or
fitting.

Stop and escalate when isolation cannot be verified, the joint is part of a
shared riser or central-heating circuit, the fitting is cracked or severely
corroded, or failure could cause uncontrolled flooding.
