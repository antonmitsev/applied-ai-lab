# Compression Fitting

> Draft canonical unit. “Compression fitting” describes the joining method; the
> exact ferrule, seat, pipe support, and seal geometry vary by product and pipe
> material.

## Short description

A compression fitting joins a pipe to a fitting body by tightening a nut that
compresses a ring, ferrule, olive, or other sealing element around the pipe and
against a designed seat. The compressed element can provide both retention and
sealing, but the exact mechanism depends on the fitting and pipe system.

The nut thread normally transfers the tightening force. It is not automatically
the water-sealing surface.

## Components

A typical compression assembly may contain:

- a fitting body with a pipe entry and an internal seat;
- a compression nut;
- a ferrule, olive, sleeve, or formed compression ring;
- the pipe end;
- a tube insert or stiffener for some plastic or multilayer pipes;
- a shoulder, stop, guide, or support element;
- an optional separate gasket or elastomeric seal in product-specific designs.

The ferrule and insert must not be assumed interchangeable between copper,
plastic, and multilayer pipe systems. Confirm the fitting specification and pipe
type before assembly.

## How the mechanical connection is created

The pipe is prepared to the specified length and condition, inserted fully into
the fitting body, and supported by any required insert or stop. The nut is
started without cross-threading and tightened so that the ferrule is driven
against the body seat and around the pipe.

The fitting body, nut, ferrule, and pipe form the mechanical load path. The
ferrule may grip or deform onto the pipe while the nut maintains compression.
The pipe must be aligned and supported so that bending loads do not unload the
seal or pull the fitting sideways.

## What actually seals

In a conventional compression design, sealing is created at the compressed
interfaces between the ferrule, pipe, and fitting seat. Some products use an
elastomeric ring or a different formed element; that product-specific element
then provides the relevant fluid barrier.

The thread supplies engagement and axial force. The correct seal depends on the
matching body, nut, ferrule, pipe dimensions, insertion depth, surface
condition, and tightening method.

## What does not seal

Do not assume that the following seals a compression fitting:

- the nut thread;
- thread tape or paste on the compression thread;
- a ferrule from a different fitting family;
- a pipe that is only partly inserted;
- an improvised washer placed under the nut;
- extra tightening after a damaged or reused ferrule has failed.

The compression thread must remain able to transfer the intended clamping
force. Sealant on that thread can interfere with assembly, contaminate the
system, or hide a geometry problem.

## Permitted movement and tightening

Before tightening, the pipe should enter the fitting straight and reach the
designed stop or insertion depth. The nut should engage smoothly by hand for
the initial turns.

After assembly, the pipe should not rotate, pull out, or move enough to disturb
the ferrule under normal service. Provide appropriate support for long, heavy,
hot, or vibrating pipework.

Tightening must follow the product-specific instruction when available. Do not
invent a torque value, use unlimited force, or assume that a second tightening
will restore a ferrule that has already been deformed or damaged.

## Typical failures

Typical failure modes include:

- pipe not inserted to the designed depth;
- wrong pipe outside diameter, wall type, or material;
- missing or incorrect tube insert for a plastic or multilayer pipe;
- ferrule, nut, or body from an incompatible fitting family;
- reused, cracked, displaced, or damaged ferrule;
- pipe end cut at an angle, burred, ovalized, scratched, or contaminated;
- fitting body or seat damaged by tools, corrosion, or debris;
- under-tightening, over-tightening, or incorrect assembly sequence;
- pipe movement, bending, vibration, or thermal expansion loading the fitting;
- a nearby leak mistaken for a compression-joint leak.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms include:

- a drip at the nut or pipe entry;
- leakage after the joint has been disturbed or reassembled;
- leakage that changes when the nut is tightened or the pipe is supported;
- a pipe that rotates or withdraws when it should be retained;
- a visible ferrule that is tilted, displaced, or not seated as expected;
- repeated leakage after replacing only the nut or adding thread sealant.

The first wet point may be at the pipe entry while water travels from a damaged
seat or a nearby connection. Trace the source before changing the ferrule.

## Diagnosis

When isolation and depressurization are safe:

1. Isolate the relevant branch and remove stored pressure and heat.
2. Dry the fitting and identify the first wet point under the lowest safe test
   condition.
3. Record the pipe material, outside diameter, fitting markings, and whether an
   insert, ferrule, or separate gasket is visible.
4. Without applying extra force, observe whether pipe support or movement changes
   the symptom.
5. If disassembly is justified, preserve the order of the nut, ferrule, insert,
   and any washers. Inspect the pipe end, seat, ferrule, threads, and debris.
6. Verify the replacement parts against the same fitting family and pipe
   specification. Visual similarity is not sufficient.
7. Reassemble only when the insertion depth, alignment, support, and tightening
   method are known.

If the pipe type, fitting family, isolation state, or required insert is
uncertain, stop before applying more tightening force.

## Assembly errors

Common errors include:

- failing to cut and prepare the pipe squarely;
- omitting the required tube insert or using the wrong insert;
- placing the ferrule in the wrong orientation;
- inserting the pipe only partially;
- contaminating the seat or pipe with chips, dirt, or old seal material;
- starting the nut at an angle or cross-threading it;
- adding thread sealant to the compression thread;
- holding the fitting body incorrectly while tightening the nut;
- using the fitting to pull misaligned or unsupported pipework into position;
- reusing a ferrule that is designed for one assembly or has permanent damage.

## Safety

Isolate and depressurize the branch before loosening a compression nut. Protect
against hot water, stored pressure, sudden pipe release, and discharge near
electrical equipment.

Stop and escalate when the isolation point is uncertain, the pipe or fitting is
cracked, the joint is part of a shared riser or central-heating circuit, the
pipe cannot be supported, or failure could cause uncontrolled flooding.
